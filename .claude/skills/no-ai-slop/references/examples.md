# Before/After Examples

## Example 1: Throat-Clearing + Binary Contrast

**Before:**
> Here's the thing: building products is hard. Not because the technology is complex. Because people are complex. Let that sink in.

**After:**
> Building products is hard not because the technology is complex but because people are unpredictable.

**What changed:** Removed the opener ("Here's the thing") and the emphasis crutch ("Let that sink in"). Joined the binary contrast into a single sentence with "not because... but because..." instead of splitting it across two choppy sentences. One flowing sentence does the work of four.

---

## Example 2: Staccato Sentences + Enumeration

**Before:**
> One cofounder wants to pivot. Another wants to cut costs. A third wants to bring in an investor who demands different strategy. Founders can handle hardship but struggle when aligned ambition fractures.

**After:**
> Cofounders split over direction, with one pushing to pivot while another wants to cut costs and a third wants outside investment. That fracture is harder to survive than the hardship itself.

**What changed:** Three parallel sentences ("One... Another... A third...") combined into one flowing sentence with conjunctions. The punchy closer was folded into a connected thought.

---

## Example 3: Business Jargon + Filler

**Before:**
> In today's fast-paced landscape, we need to lean into discomfort and navigate uncertainty with clarity. This matters because your competition isn't waiting.

**After:**
> Move faster. Your competitors are.

**What changed:** Stripped out every piece of jargon ("landscape", "lean into", "navigate"). The core message needed six words, not thirty.

---

## Example 4: Inline Colons + Short Sentences

**Before:**
> The path forward requires three things: focus, speed, and trust. We simplify. We focus. We execute.

**After:**
> The path forward requires focus, speed, and trust, and the only way to get there is by simplifying how the team works.

**What changed:** Replaced the inline colon with a natural sentence flow. Joined the three staccato fragments ("We simplify. We focus. We execute.") into a connected clause. Colons should only appear before bullet-point or numbered lists.

---

## Example 5: Staccato Join (fluency score 5, join works)

**Before:**
> The team shipped late. The client noticed. Trust eroded.

**After:**
> The team shipped late and the client noticed, which eroded trust fast.

**What changed:** Three staccato sentences joined with conjunctions. The joined version flows naturally (score 5) because the ideas follow a clear cause-and-effect chain: late shipment → client noticed → trust eroded. No rephrase needed.

---

## Example 6: Staccato Rephrase (fluency score 2, join fails)

**Before:**
> Cowork sessions were stateless. Projects make them cumulative. The difference between a tool you use and a tool that learns how you work.

**After (bad join, score 2):**
> Cowork sessions were stateless, and Projects make them cumulative, the difference between a tool you use and one that learns how you work.

**After (rephrase, score 5):**
> Cowork sessions were stateless, but Projects remember what came before, turning a tool you use into one that learns how you work.

**What changed:** The mechanical join produced three comma-separated clauses with shifting subjects and no clear connective, reading like stapled fragments. The rephrase preserves the same meaning (stateless vs. cumulative, tool vs. learning tool) but uses "but" for contrast and "turning" to fold the third idea into a participial phrase that flows from the second.

---

## Example 7: Staccato Join (fluency score 4, join works)

**Before:**
> Revenue climbed. Costs dropped. The board approved the next round.

**After:**
> Revenue climbed while costs dropped, so the board approved the next round.

**What changed:** Three short sentences joined with "while" (for the simultaneous contrast) and "so" (for the consequence). Scores 4 because the logical links between ideas are clear: revenue up + costs down → board approval. The conjunctions do real work here, not just stitching.

---

## Example 8: Staccato Rephrase (fluency score 2, join fails)

**Before:**
> The API is fast. The documentation is sparse. Developers avoid it.

**After (bad join, score 2):**
> The API is fast, the documentation is sparse, and developers avoid it.

**After (rephrase, score 5):**
> Developers avoid the API despite its speed because the documentation barely exists.

**What changed:** The mechanical join produced a flat comma-list of three unrelated-sounding clauses. The rephrase leads with the consequence (developers avoid it), uses "despite" to acknowledge the positive (fast API), and "because" to name the real cause (sparse docs). Same three facts, but now they read as one argument instead of three bullet points on a line.

---

## Example 9: False Agency + Passive Voice (originally Example 5)

**Before:**
> The conversation around remote work has shifted. What was once seen as a temporary measure has become a permanent fixture. The data tells us that productivity hasn't suffered.

**After:**
> Companies kept remote work because their teams stayed productive, and managers who fought the change lost that argument.

**What changed:** Named actual actors (companies, teams, managers) instead of letting "the conversation" and "the data" do human things. Active voice throughout, and two sentences became one with a conjunction.

---

## Example 10: Narrator-from-a-Distance + Vague Declaratives (originally Example 6)

**Before:**
> Nobody designed this system to fail. But the implications are significant. People tend to underestimate how deeply structural these problems are.

**After:**
> The hiring pipeline breaks at the phone screen stage because recruiters take three weeks to respond, and fixing the timeline would recover 40% of lost candidates.

**What changed:** Replaced floating observations with specific details. Named the system, the failure point, and the fix. The reader can evaluate the claim instead of being told things are "significant."
